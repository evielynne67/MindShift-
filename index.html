01 / Brain science

Meet the loop
behind the scroll.

Every notification, like, and new video can give your brain a small dopamine signal. Dopamine is not “the addiction chemical”; it helps your brain learn what might be worth repeating.

When the reward is unpredictable, your brain checks again. That is why stopping can feel harder when you are tired or stressed. Understanding the loop is a kinder and more useful first step than blaming yourself.

03 / Screens & tech

Build a softer
off-ramp.

Your phone is designed to keep you engaged. You do not need perfect discipline to change the pattern. Add friction, lower the brightness, and swap the last scroll for something that helps your body slow down.

  • ✓Set a gentle boundary: charge your phone away from your pillow.
  • ✓Make the next action obvious: keep a book, water, or notebook nearby.
  • ✓Expect imperfect nights: consistency grows from returning, not restarting.

A practical next step

Make one small change
that fits your life.

Progress is easier to repeat when the next step is clear and realistic. Choose one idea to test, then adjust it to what works for you.

01 / SLEEP

Protect a wind-down window

Pick a bedtime routine you can repeat: dim the lights, plug your phone in away from the bed, and give yourself time to settle. Teenagers generally need 8–10 hours of sleep.

02 / SCREENS

Add a stopping cue

Choose a natural endpoint, such as finishing one video or setting an evening alarm. Put the next activity where you can see it: a book, music, or tomorrow’s checklist.

03 / SUPPORT

Say what is going on

If stress or low mood keeps affecting daily life, tell a trusted adult, school counsellor, or health professional. You do not need to diagnose yourself before asking for help.
